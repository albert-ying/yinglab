Our vision

We want to understand aging well enough to extend healthy human lifespan by an order of magnitude.

Evolution has already done more than that. A mouse lives 4 years and a bowhead whale more than 200. Current interventions change lifespan by a small fraction, so the mechanisms behind the larger differences are probably ones they do not reach.

We also ask what aging is. Gerophysics treats the organism as a physical system that keeps itself stable, and aging as the loss of that stability; the theory tells the models what to look for.

The lab is computational and experimental. Models and agents propose interventions, experiments decide, and the results train the next model.

AI for biology, across scales

Most AI for biology works at the smallest scales, on protein structure and virtual cells. Aging shows itself in the whole organism: in disease, in death and in the lifespan of a species. We model every scale, and much of our work starts at the human and the species, where few models exist.

Brain resilience

Aging is the largest risk factor for Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease, yet some people keep their memory into old age even when their brains carry the pathology. We want to know what protects them.

We expect the longest-lived species to have the most resilient brains. A bowhead whale can live more than two centuries, a Greenland shark an estimated four, and a naked mole-rat decades longer than a rodent of its size. We compare their brains with human brains to find protective molecules and cell states.

In humans, we model brain aging from molecular profiles of brain tissue, blood and cerebrospinal fluid. Resilience becomes measurable: how far a person's course departs from the one expected from their age, pathology and genetic risk. What explains that difference becomes a target.

AI agents that read data and papers

Public repositories hold millions of molecular profiles and the literature millions of papers, most analyzed only for the question of the study that produced them. Our agents read both, run the analyses and propose what to test, and every claim keeps a record of the data, code and paper behind it.

In ClockBase Agent, agents reanalyzed public profiles with aging clocks and nominated interventions predicted to lower biological age. One of them, ouabain, reduced frailty in aged mice.

Research

Every project runs one loop: measure a biological state, model it, let the model nominate an intervention, design the molecule, test it and feed the result back. The first projects start in six areas.

Gerophysics

Aging from first principles. An organism holds its state stable through constant repair, and aging is the slow drift of that state towards the point where stability is lost.

  • Loss of resilience: slower recovery and larger fluctuations with age
  • Scaling laws that link lifespan to body size, metabolism and repair across species
  • Mathematical models of aging checked against data from many species

Foundation and world models of biology

A world model predicts how a biological state changes over time and in response to an intervention.

  • Foundation models of molecular state across omics
  • World models of the aging cell
  • Pan-species and evolutionary models of longevity

Measuring and modeling aging

  • Biological age and module-specific aging signatures
  • Aging and resilience trajectories
  • Causal aging biomarkers

AI agents for scientific discovery

  • Autonomous discovery of interventions from public data
  • Agent workflows in which every step can be inspected
  • Synthesis of public datasets and the literature

Generative therapeutic design

  • De novo protein design for protein aggregation and neurodegeneration
  • Evolution-informed protein engineering
  • Experimental validation of targets proposed by the models

Disease areas

  • Brain aging, neurodegeneration and brain resilience, the lab's first system
  • Cancer and other age-related diseases, studied together
The model is the instrument
We test a hypothesis first in a pretrained model of biological state and take to the bench the predictions that hold up there.
Results trace to their source
For each candidate we keep the source data, the code, the model output and the literature search that support it.
The loop closes on experiments
A prediction about aging can be checked against physiological function and lifespan, and we hold every project to that test.

    Kejun Albert Ying, PhD

    Principal investigator. Incoming Assistant Professor.

    Albert works on AI for science, with an emphasis on AI for biology and aging. He develops foundation models and world models for biological data and builds agentic systems for discovery. His earlier work includes causality-enriched aging clocks, ClockBase and MethylGPT.

    He did his postdoctoral research at Stanford University and the University of Washington, co-advised by Tony Wyss-Coray and David Baker, as an NIH/NIA F99/K00 fellow. He received his PhD from Harvard University in 2025, advised by Vadim Gladyshev, with the dissertation On the Quantification of Aging, and a master's degree in computational science and engineering from Harvard.

    He is a core member of the Biomarkers of Aging Consortium, an organizer of the Biomarkers of Aging Challenge, and a recipient of the 2026 WAIC Yunfan Award.
